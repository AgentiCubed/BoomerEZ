# 15-Minute Moderated Usability Test for First-Time Users Aged 65+

## Purpose

Use this protocol to evaluate whether a first-time user aged 65 or older can understand, complete, and verify a core product flow without moderator instruction.

This is a reusable template. Replace the following fields before the session:

- `[product]`: Product or service being tested.
- `[core goal]`: The outcome the participant wants, stated in everyday language.
- `[starting screen]`: The same starting point for every participant.
- `[test information]`: Fictional, non-sensitive data supplied for the task.
- `[success state]`: The visible state that proves completion.
- `[detail]` and `[replacement]`: Information used in the change or recovery task.
- `[organization]`, `[researcher]`, `[contact]`, `[recording type]`, and `[retention period]`: Study-administration details.

The consent language is a practical template, not legal or institutional-review advice. Adapt it to the organization's privacy, research, and accessibility requirements.

## Research Objectives

Determine whether a first-time participant can:

1. Recognize where to begin without being shown.
2. Understand the language, controls, and consequences of the primary flow.
3. Complete the core action using test data.
4. Recover from or change one detail.
5. Recognize that the action succeeded.
6. Use the experience with their normal accessibility settings or normal caregiver support.

### Primary Research Questions

- Can the participant choose a plausible first action?
- Which labels, controls, or screen changes are misunderstood?
- Do motor or visual demands prevent a participant who understands the task from executing it?
- Does uncertainty create delay, reassurance-seeking, or abandonment?
- Does confidence hide a wrong mental model?
- Does the product support a real participant-caregiver division of labor?
- What evidence convinces the participant that the task is complete?

## Study Design at a Glance

| Item | Plan |
| --- | --- |
| Audience | Adults aged 65+, using `[product]` for the first time |
| Session | Moderated, 15 minutes of active session time |
| Format | In person or remote; use the participant's familiar device when possible |
| Core tasks | Find the path, complete the action, change or verify the result |
| Moderator stance | Neutral, non-instructional, patient, and nonjudgmental |
| Accessibility | Usual glasses, magnification, screen reader, hearing aids, input devices, and other normal settings remain available |
| Caregiver | Use either independent mode or natural-support mode, declared before the first task |
| Evidence | Behavior, words, errors, time, assistance, and visible task state |

Five participants—especially one per persona type—can reveal serious usability failures, but cannot establish how common those failures are. Use additional participants before making prevalence claims.

# Screener

## Recruiter Introduction

> We are inviting people to try `[product]` for the first time and tell us what is clear or confusing. This is a test of the product, not a test of you. The session lasts about 15 minutes. You do not need to be good with technology, and you do not need to disclose a medical diagnosis. I will ask a few questions to see whether the study is a fit and whether we should arrange any accommodations.

## Eligibility

### Required

- Age 65 or older.
- Has never used the exact product or tested version.
- Can provide informed consent personally.
- Can participate for approximately 15 minutes with requested accommodations.
- Is willing to use fictional test information rather than private personal information.

### Exclude or Review

- Has previously used, designed, sold, supported, or tested `[product]`.
- Works for `[organization]`, a direct competitor, or in usability or market research, unless that background is intentionally in scope.
- Cannot consent personally. A caregiver cannot consent on behalf of an adult for this lightweight study unless the research has a separately approved legal and ethics process.
- Requires an accommodation the team cannot yet provide. Do not silently exclude; record the gap and determine whether it can be resolved.

Do not exclude someone merely because they use assistive technology, have a tremor, have low vision, lack confidence, or normally receive help. Those characteristics are central to this study.

## Screener Questions and Routing

### S1. Age

> Which age range are you in?

- Under 65 — thank and end screening.
- 65–69.
- 70–74.
- 75–79.
- 80–84.
- 85 or older.
- Prefer not to say — review only if age eligibility can be confirmed another way.

### S2. Prior Use

> Before today, have you ever used `[product]`, including a trial, demonstration, or earlier research session?

- No — continue.
- Yes — exclude from the first-time-user study.
- Not sure — ask what they remember; exclude if they interacted with the same core flow.

### S3. Possible Professional Bias

> Do you currently work, or have you recently worked, for `[organization]`, a similar company, or in product design, software testing, user research, or market research?

- No — continue.
- Yes — review against the study's exclusion rule.

### S4. Familiar Device Use

> Which devices do you use at least once a week? Select all that apply.

- Smartphone.
- Tablet.
- Laptop or desktop computer.
- None of these.

Follow-up:

> Which one would you normally use for `[domain activity]`?

Recruit on the device the person would naturally choose whenever possible. Device frequency is descriptive, not automatically an exclusion.

### S5. Confidence With an Unfamiliar Product

> If you had to use a new app or website without anyone showing you first, how confident would you feel?

1. Not at all confident.
2. Slightly confident.
3. Moderately confident.
4. Very confident.
5. Completely confident.

- Scores 1–2 may qualify for the low-confidence cell.
- Scores 4–5 may qualify for the high-confidence cell.

### S6. Product Vocabulary Familiarity

Choose five or six terms that actually appear in the tested flow. Do not define them during screening.

> Which of these words would you feel comfortable explaining to a friend: `[term 1]`, `[term 2]`, `[term 3]`, `[term 4]`, `[term 5]`, and `[term 6]`?

For each selected term, ask:

> In this kind of product, what would you expect that word to mean?

Score only for recruitment and analysis; do not tell the participant that an answer is right or wrong.

- High confidence at S5 plus accurate understanding of zero to two relevant terms may qualify for the high-confidence, low-product-vocabulary cell.
- Measure product vocabulary, not intelligence, education, general literacy, accent, or English fluency.

### S7. Motor Access

> When using a phone, tablet, mouse, or keyboard, do small targets, precise movements, dragging, or repeated taps take extra effort because of hand movement, pain, stiffness, or limited dexterity?

- Never.
- Sometimes.
- Often.
- Prefer not to say.

Follow-up if sometimes or often:

> What do you normally do to make the device easier to use?

The answer may qualify the person for the tremor or motor-precision cell. A diagnosis is not required.

### S8. Visual Access

> Even with your usual glasses or contact lenses, do you change text size, zoom, magnification, contrast, color, or use a screen reader to use a phone or computer?

- No.
- Sometimes.
- Usually.
- Prefer not to say.

Follow-up:

> Which settings or tools do you normally use, and on which device?

The answer may qualify the person for the low-vision cell. Arrange the session so those normal tools are available.

### S9. Hearing and Communication Access

> Is there anything we should arrange so that you can hear, understand, or respond comfortably—for example captions, a quieter room, more time to answer, or written task cards?

Record the requested accommodation. This question supports access; it is not a target-persona classification.

### S10. Normal Help From Another Person

> When you do something like `[domain activity]`, how often does another person help you?

- Never.
- Rarely.
- Sometimes.
- Usually.
- Always.

If sometimes, usually, or always:

> Who helps, and which parts do they usually handle?

Examples may include finding the service, signing in, receiving a verification code, reading choices, entering information, making the decision, paying, or saving confirmation details.

- Recruit for the caregiver-mediated cell when shared use is normal, not staged for the study.
- Invite the usual helper only with the participant's permission.

### S11. Recording Preference

> The researcher would like to record `[recording type]` so the team can review what happened. Would you be comfortable deciding about that after hearing the full consent explanation?

- Yes.
- No; notes only.
- Unsure; discuss at the session.

Do not require recording unless it is genuinely essential and disclosed in the recruitment materials.

### S12. Accommodations and Availability

> What would make a 15-minute session comfortable and accessible for you?

> Are you available at `[times]`?

Confirm compensation, location or remote link, device, caregiver attendance, and accessibility arrangements in plain language.

## Recruitment Cells

| Cell | Primary screener signal | Keep distinct from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tremor or motor precision | S7 = sometimes or often; participant knows what they want to do but precise input is effortful | Product comprehension or low confidence |
| Low vision | S8 = sometimes or usually; uses visual access settings or tools | Memory or general navigation ability |
| Low confidence | S5 = 1–2, without a primary visual or motor barrier | Inability to understand the flow |
| High confidence, low product vocabulary | S5 = 4–5 plus weak understanding of product-relevant terms at S6 | Carelessness or general literacy |
| Caregiver-mediated | S10 = sometimes, usually, or always; shared work is normal | Lack of capacity or lack of agency |

A participant may fit more than one cell. Assign a primary analytic lens, record all relevant access needs, and do not erase intersections to make the sample look cleaner.

# Consent Script

Send accessible written consent in advance when possible, then read this short confirmation before recording or showing the product.

## Participant Script

> Thank you for joining. My name is `[researcher]`, and I am working with `[organization]`.
>
> Today we are testing `[product]`, not testing you. I did not design everything you will see, so honest criticism is useful and will not offend me. The session will take about 15 minutes.
>
> I will ask you to try a few activities and say what you are looking for, what you expect, and anything that seems unclear. I will not be able to show you how to complete the activities because we need to see what the product communicates on its own.
>
> Taking part is voluntary. You may skip any question, take a break, or stop at any time without giving a reason and without losing your compensation. Please do not enter real passwords, payment details, medical information, or other private information; use only the test information I provide.
>
> We would like to collect `[notes / audio / video / screen recording]` for `[research purpose]`. The research team will limit access to `[authorized people]`, retain it for `[retention period]`, and report findings without your name unless you separately agree otherwise. If you prefer not to be recorded, `[state the notes-only option or explain that recording is required]`.
>
> This is not a medical assessment, and you do not need to explain or prove any health condition. You may use your normal glasses, magnification, screen reader, hearing aids, input device, or other accessibility settings.
>
> What questions do you have before we begin?

Ask each consent question separately:

1. > Do you agree to participate?
2. > Do you agree to audio recording?
3. > Do you agree to screen or video recording?
4. > May we use short anonymous quotations in our internal report?

Record each answer. Begin recording only after the relevant permission is given.

## Caregiver Addendum

Address the participant first, then the caregiver or support person.

> `[Participant name]` is the primary participant and decision-maker in this session. I will ask them to answer first. We are also interested in how the two of you normally divide the work, so we will use the support mode we agree on before the tasks. Either of you may stop the session. Please do not share real private information.

Ask:

> `[Participant name]`, are you comfortable with `[caregiver name]` staying and participating in the way we just described?

> `[Caregiver name]`, do you agree to the same recording and privacy terms?

Consent from a caregiver does not replace the participant's consent.

# Moderator Preparation

Complete these checks before the participant joins:

- Replace every bracketed placeholder.
- Prepare a test account and fictional data; remove any need for real passwords, financial details, or health information.
- Reset the product to `[starting screen]` and verify that the core flow works.
- Turn off product tours, sample hints, or seeded state unless they are part of the experience being evaluated.
- Prepare large-print task cards in at least 18-point type with high contrast.
- Make captions, audio controls, lighting, seating, and a stable device surface available.
- Confirm that the participant's normal zoom, magnification, screen reader, stylus, mouse, keyboard, or other input method can be used.
- Hide observer video and mute observers. Observers communicate only with the moderator through a private channel.
- Start a visible clock for the moderator, not a countdown for the participant.
- Decide which caregiver mode applies and record it before Task 1.

Accessibility is not coaching. Letting a participant use their normal access settings gives them access to the same task; pointing out the correct control gives them the answer.

# Fifteen-Minute Session Protocol

## Run of Show

| Time | Activity | Moderator objective |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00–1:45 | Welcome and consent confirmation | Establish safety, voluntariness, recording permissions, and “we test the product” framing |
| 1:45–2:30 | Access and caregiver-mode check | Put normal settings and support arrangement in place before measuring product use |
| 2:30–3:00 | Think-aloud instruction | Explain the participant's role without demonstrating the product |
| 3:00–5:00 | Task 1: Find the path | Measure first-action clarity and discoverability |
| 5:00–9:30 | Task 2: Complete the core action | Measure comprehension, execution, errors, and success recognition |
| 9:30–12:00 | Task 3: Change, recover, or verify | Measure control, reversibility, and trust in system status |
| 12:00–14:20 | Debrief | Understand expectations, uncertainty, assistance, language, and priority change |
| 14:20–15:00 | Close | Stop recording, explain next steps, and confirm compensation |

Schedule a buffer after the 15-minute appointment so the participant is never visibly rushed. If consent or an accommodation takes longer, shorten the task set rather than shortening consent or pressuring the participant.

## 0:00–1:45 — Welcome and Consent

Read the consent script. Confirm each permission. Do not record before permission.

## 1:45–2:30 — Access and Support Check

Say:

> Before we start, would you like to adjust anything so this is comfortable to see, hear, or control? Please use the glasses, settings, or tools you would normally use at home.

If a caregiver is present, select one mode:

- **Independent baseline:** The caregiver remains silent during tasks and may help only for safety or a technical problem outside the product. Use when the research question is independent use.
- **Natural-support mode:** The pair divides work as they normally would. The moderator does not coach either person. Record who notices, decides, touches the device, supplies credentials, and confirms success. Use when the research question is real caregiver-mediated use.

Do not switch modes mid-task without recording the switch and the reason.

## 2:30–3:00 — Think-Aloud Instruction

Say:

> As you work, please say what you are looking for, what you think something means, and what you expect to happen. You do not have to keep talking every second. If you become quiet, I may ask what you are thinking. I cannot tell you how to do the activity, but there are no wrong comments.

## 3:00–12:00 — Tasks

Read each task exactly as written. Place the large-print task card where the participant can consult it. Start timing after the participant finishes reading or hearing the task.

After each task, ask:

> Overall, was that very easy, somewhat easy, neither easy nor difficult, somewhat difficult, or very difficult?

Record the answer without asking the participant to justify it unless time allows.

# Task List

## Task 1 — Find Where to Begin

### Participant Prompt

> Imagine you want to `[core goal]`. Starting from this screen, show me what you would do first. Stop when you believe you have reached the right place to begin.

### Researcher-Only Success Criteria

- Participant identifies the correct entry point without moderator or caregiver instruction.
- Participant can describe what they expect the next step to accomplish.
- “Correct” is based on the product's intended path, not the moderator's preferred gesture or device.

### Observe

- First action and whether it is correct.
- Labels the participant reads or ignores.
- Scanning pattern, zooming, panning, accidental activation, hesitation, and backtracking.
- Difference between what the participant says they intend and what their hand or pointer activates.

### Stop Rule

Stop after two minutes, a clear correct entry, or a critical dead end. Say:

> Thank you. I am going to stop this task here and move us to the next one.

Do not reveal the correct path. Reset the product neutrally if necessary.

## Task 2 — Complete the Core Action

### Participant Prompt

> Using the test information on this card, please `[core goal]`. Continue until you believe you are finished.

Avoid task wording that repeats a control label. For example, if the button says “Create request,” the task should describe the desired outcome rather than say “Create a request.”

### Researcher-Only Success Criteria

- Participant reaches `[success state]` with correct test information.
- Participant avoids an irreversible, unsafe, or privacy-compromising action.
- Participant recognizes completion without moderator confirmation.

### Observe

- Whether terms, icons, field formats, permissions, or warnings are understood.
- Input errors versus comprehension errors.
- Attempts to seek reassurance before acting.
- Fast but incorrect choices made with confidence.
- Whether the participant notices validation errors and can recover.
- Whether a caregiver becomes the operator, and at which step.
- Evidence the participant uses to decide the task is complete.

### Stop Rule

Stop after four and a half minutes, successful completion, an unsafe action, or two repeated cycles through the same dead end.

## Task 3 — Change, Recover, or Verify

Use the version that fits the product.

### Version A: Change and Confirm

> You notice that `[detail]` should be `[replacement]`. Please change it. Then show me how you would make sure the change took effect.

### Version B: Recover

> Imagine you selected the wrong `[item or option]`. Show me what you would do to correct it without starting over, if possible.

### Version C: Verify Later

> Imagine you returned tomorrow and wanted to make sure `[core goal]` was completed. Show me where you would look.

### Researcher-Only Success Criteria

- Participant finds the relevant edit, undo, history, status, or confirmation path.
- Participant completes the correction or identifies the saved result.
- Participant can point to visible evidence that the state is correct.

### Observe

- Discoverability of edit, back, undo, history, help, or status controls.
- Fear of losing progress or causing an irreversible action.
- Whether confirmation language is understandable.
- Whether zoom, focus, or page reflow separates the result from the action.
- Whether the product requires a caregiver credential or device during recovery.

### Stop Rule

Stop after two and a half minutes, successful verification, or a repeated dead end.

# Standard Non-Coaching Rules

## Core Rule

The moderator may protect consent, safety, dignity, access, and study equipment. The moderator may not supply information that the interface is supposed to communicate.

## Neutral Prompt Ladder

Use the least intrusive prompt and wait before moving to the next:

1. Wait silently for 5–10 seconds.
2. > What are you thinking right now?
3. > What are you looking for?
4. > What do you expect that to do?
5. > What would you do if you were at home?
6. > Is there anything else you would try?
7. End the task neutrally when the time or safety limit is reached.

Do not move down the ladder merely because the participant is slower than the moderator expected.

## Do Not

- Point at, look toward, hover over, highlight, or name the correct control.
- Repeat a key word that appears only on the correct control.
- Tell the participant what a product term, icon, color, or message means.
- Suggest an action sequence, even indirectly.
- Touch the participant's device or take control of the pointer.
- Say “yes,” “right,” “good,” “exactly,” or otherwise signal correctness.
- Say “no,” correct an error, wince, or signal that the participant chose poorly.
- Reassure the participant that a risky-looking product action is safe unless there is a genuine safety issue.
- Teach the correct workflow between tasks; that contaminates later first-use behavior.
- Let observers ask questions, react visibly, or send hints to the participant.
- Blame age, memory, confidence, disability, or “technical ability” for an observed product failure.

Use neutral acknowledgments such as “Thank you,” “I understand,” and “Please keep telling me what you are thinking.”

## Standard Responses to Participant Questions

| Participant asks | Moderator responds |
| --- | --- |
| “Is this the right one?” | “What would you expect it to do?” |
| “What should I press?” | “What would you do if you were at home?” |
| “What does this word mean?” | “What do you think it means here?” |
| “Did I finish?” | “What on the screen would tell you that?” |
| “Can my daughter help?” | Independent baseline: “For this task, please try what you would do before asking for help.” Natural-support mode: “Please work together as you normally would.” |
| “Am I taking too long?” | “No. Take the time you need; the product is what we are evaluating.” |

## Permitted Intervention

Intervene when:

- Consent, privacy, emotional comfort, physical safety, or device safety is at risk.
- The participant is about to enter real private information.
- A study-system failure—not a product usability issue—blocks the session.
- An agreed accessibility accommodation has stopped working.
- The participant asks to pause or stop.

Explain and log any intervention. If it reveals how to use the product, mark that task as moderator-assisted.

## Caregiver Conduct

- In independent-baseline mode, ask the caregiver not to point, answer, reinterpret, or take the device. Record spontaneous help before reminding them once.
- In natural-support mode, do not restrain normal help. Treat the timing, content, and ownership of each intervention as study data.
- Ask the participant for their view before asking the caregiver.
- Do not let a caregiver answer consent, preference, confidence, or comprehension questions for a participant who can answer personally.
- If the caregiver and participant disagree, record both accounts without deciding who is correct during the session.

## Per-Task Record

| Field | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Completion | Independent / caregiver-assisted / moderator-assisted / incomplete / stopped for safety |
| Time | Seconds from end of task prompt to stop rule |
| First action | Exact visible action; correct / incorrect / ambiguous |
| Critical errors | Action that blocks completion, risks harm or privacy, or creates the wrong final state |
| Other errors | Accidental activation, wrong path, format error, missed message, backtrack, repeated loop |
| Assistance | Who helped, when, what they said or did, and whether help is normal outside the study |
| Observable effort | Long pause, repeated reading, panning, zooming, hand repositioning, repeated taps, reassurance request |
| Words used | Participant's exact interpretation of labels or status messages |
| Success evidence | What the participant used—or failed to use—to decide the action worked |
| Ease rating | Very easy / somewhat easy / neither / somewhat difficult / very difficult |

## Severity

- **Critical:** Prevents the core outcome, produces a harmful or wrong result, exposes private information, or cannot be recovered without outside help.
- **Serious:** Requires caregiver or moderator help, causes repeated failure, or makes the participant likely to abandon the task.
- **Minor:** Creates delay or avoidable effort but does not threaten completion or trust.

Write observations before interpretations. “Tapped the adjacent link three times” is evidence; “was confused because of age” is unsupported interpretation.

# Debrief Interview Guide

Use the first five questions in order. Ask optional probes only when time remains or when they clarify an observed behavior.

## Core Questions: 12:00–14:20

1. > What, if anything, felt clear or easy? What made it clear?
2. > Where did you feel most unsure or have to work the hardest?
3. > Was there a point where what happened was different from what you expected?
4. > If you had to do this at home, would you continue on your own, ask someone for help, contact the company, or stop? Tell me what would lead you to that choice.
5. > If the team could change one thing before you used this again, what should it change first?

## Optional Neutral Probes

- > You paused at `[observed moment]`. What were you considering?
- > What did the word or symbol `[label]` mean to you?
- > What told you that the action had—or had not—worked?
- > How was it to read and move around this screen?
- > How was it to select and enter information on this screen?
- > What felt reversible, and what felt permanent?
- > Which part, if any, would you want another person to handle?
- > What would you need to feel comfortable doing this without help?

Avoid leading questions such as “Was the text too small?” or “Was that button confusing?” Begin with the participant's experience, then probe a specific observation.

## Caregiver Debrief

Ask after the participant answers:

1. > How did the two of you decide who would handle each part?
2. > Was that the same division of work you use at home?
3. > Where did the product make it easier or harder to help without taking over?
4. > Did either person have to use the other's device, account, code, or private information?

# Five-Person Older-Adult Persona Panel

## Shared Persona Spine

All five personas:

- Are aged 65 or older.
- Are motivated to achieve the same `[core goal]`.
- Are using `[product]` for the first time.
- Use a device that is already familiar to them.
- Can understand the consent process and make their own study decisions.
- Should not be treated as less intelligent, less independent, or less capable because of age.

Only the primary interaction constraint changes. This makes the failure signatures meaningfully different rather than five versions of “an older person gets confused.” These are hypothesis personas for recruiting, task design, and analysis—not substitutes for sessions with real people.

## Differentiation Matrix

| Persona | Primary mismatch | Characteristic failure signature | Common false conclusion | Product risk exposed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nora — tremor | Precise input demands exceed reliable motor control | Correctly explains the intended action but activates an adjacent or repeated control | “She did not understand the screen” | Small targets, destructive adjacency, drag-only controls, double submission, timeouts |
| Luis — low vision | Visual presentation fails under low vision or magnification | Searches, pans, or loses context because content is faint, clipped, or separated at zoom | “He forgot where he was” | Contrast, reflow, zoom, focus, icon-only controls, obscured status |
| Elaine — low confidence | Ambiguous consequences make action feel unsafe | Identifies a plausible action but seeks permission, backs out, or abandons before committing | “She cannot use technology” | Trust, reversibility, warnings, confirmation, privacy language |
| Arthur — high confidence, low product vocabulary | Interface terminology does not match the user's mental model | Moves quickly and confidently through the wrong path or believes the wrong state is complete | “He was careless” | Jargon, ambiguous labels, misleading icons, weak feedback |
| June and Tasha — caregiver-mediated | The product assumes one independent actor, while the real task is shared | Device, codes, decisions, and data pass between people until identity or ownership breaks | “June lacks capacity” | Proxy access, consent, authentication, handoffs, privacy, notification ownership |

## Persona 1: Nora Ellis — Understands the Task, Cannot Reliably Hit It

### Profile

- Age: 71.
- Background: Retired payroll manager; uses an iPad daily for email, banking, news, and video calls.
- Confidence: High.
- Product vocabulary: Strong.
- Access context: A hand tremor makes small, tightly spaced targets and sustained dragging unreliable. She sometimes braces her wrist or uses a stylus.

### Goal and Behavior

Nora reads instructions carefully, forms the correct plan, and usually predicts what a control will do. She becomes frustrated when the product treats an imprecise tap as a decision she did not intend to make.

### Distinct Failure Mode

- Activates the control beside the intended target.
- Triggers double submission while trying to confirm a tap.
- Cannot maintain a drag gesture or hold target.
- Dismisses a message while trying to select it.
- Times out while recovering from an input error.

### Observable Signature

Her spoken intention is correct immediately before the wrong activation. Comprehension remains stable while execution fails.

### Test Hypothesis

Nora can complete `[core goal]` independently when every action has a large, separated target, a non-drag alternative, visible focus or pressed feedback, and a reversible result.

### Measures That Matter

- Unintended activation count.
- Repeated-tap or double-submit count.
- Recovery from input error.
- Completion with preferred input method.
- Whether timeout behavior penalizes slower precision.

### Moderator Caution

Do not interpret a motor error as confusion, and do not steady the device or tap for her unless she requests an accommodation that would also be available in normal use.

## Persona 2: Luis Mendoza — Confident Navigation That Breaks Under Magnification

### Profile

- Age: 77.
- Background: Former building inspector; uses a Windows laptop and Android phone for bills, maps, and community activities.
- Confidence: Moderate to high.
- Product vocabulary: Moderate.
- Access context: Low vision; normally uses 200% zoom, larger text, strong contrast, and occasional screen magnification.

### Goal and Behavior

Luis is methodical and comfortable exploring. He remembers task structure well when the screen preserves context, but magnification reduces his field of view and forces additional panning.

### Distinct Failure Mode

- A key action moves off screen or becomes clipped at 200% zoom.
- A fixed banner or keyboard covers the active field or confirmation.
- Low-contrast text or disabled-state styling appears absent.
- An icon has no readable label.
- Focus jumps after an error, causing him to lose his place.

### Observable Signature

He asks whether a control exists, pans repeatedly, or returns to content he already understood. The problem appears or worsens at his normal display settings.

### Test Hypothesis

Luis can complete `[core goal]` when the flow reflows cleanly at his normal zoom, retains context and focus, exposes text alternatives, and keeps status messages near the affected action.

### Measures That Matter

- Completion at normal zoom and contrast.
- Number of pans or zoom changes needed.
- Lost-context events after navigation or validation.
- Missed controls or messages caused by presentation.
- Ability to locate proof of success.

### Moderator Caution

Do not reduce zoom for convenience or point out content outside his current viewport. A screen that works only after removing his normal accommodation has failed the test.

## Persona 3: Elaine Parker — Understands More Than She Trusts Herself to Do

### Profile

- Age: 68.
- Background: Part-time library volunteer; uses a smartphone for messages, photos, and a few familiar services.
- Confidence: Low with unfamiliar products.
- Product vocabulary: Moderate.
- Access context: No primary visual or motor constraint. A prior scam and fear of irreversible mistakes make unfamiliar requests feel risky.

### Goal and Behavior

### Profile

- Private information exposed to the helper.

# Reporting Guardrails

- Report the interface condition and behavior, not an age-based trait: “At 200% zoom, the confirmation moved outside the visible region,” not “older users could not find confirmation.”
- Separate comprehension failure, execution failure, visibility failure, confidence or trust failure, vocabulary failure, and shared-workflow failure.
- Report assistance explicitly: independent, caregiver-assisted, moderator-assisted, or incomplete.
- Treat a participant's workaround as evidence of unmet need, not proof that the design works.
- Do not generalize from one persona representative to all adults aged 65+.
- Prioritize critical failures that threaten completion, privacy, money, health, or agency even when only one participant encounters them.
